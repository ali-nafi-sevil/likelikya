Antiphellos & Phellos

Ἀντίφελλος · Φελλός — modern Kaş, Antalya province
At a glance. Twin Lycian cities on the central coast. Phellos — the older mother city — sits on a high inland ridge above the modern village of Çukurbağ. Antiphellos — "opposite Phellos" — is the coastal port, today buried partly under the streets of Kaş and partly still walkable as ruins on the edge of town. Together they were one of the more prosperous small members of the Lycian League.

Where, exactly

Antiphellos faces south across a narrow strait at the Greek island of Meis (Kastellorizo), about two kilometres away. The town occupies a small natural amphitheatre between two rocky headlands. Phellos sits 850 metres above sea level on the limestone ridge to the north, reached today by a 1–2 hour walk up from Çukurbağ village.

A short history

Phellos is the older settlement, attested already in the 6th century BC and minting its own coinage with a boar's head in the 5th. The name comes from the Greek for "stony ground," which is exactly what the ridge is. As Mediterranean trade increased, Phellos needed a usable harbour — Antiphellos grew up as that harbour. By the Hellenistic period the daughter city had overtaken the mother in wealth and importance, becoming the more significant of the two as a member of the Lycian League.

Strabo singles Antiphellos out for its sponges — a reminder that the coast's sponge-diving industry, which lasted into the 20th century in nearby Kalkan and on Meis, is a very old one.

Archaeology

The Hellenistic theatre

The single most prominent ancient structure in modern Kaş. A small Greek-style theatre cut into the slope just west of the harbour, with about 26 rows of seats still in place and a view straight out to Meis. The orchestra is more than half-circular, an older Greek form rather than the semicircular Roman one — a clue to its early Hellenistic date.

The "Doric" rock tomb

A short walk above the theatre, a rectangular tomb is carved into the cliff face with a single chamber and a frieze of dancing women in low relief on the back wall — possibly Nereids or maenads. Inside, the long stone benches where the dead were laid are still in place.

The Lion Sarcophagus

Standing in the middle of Uzun Çarşı, the long uphill street in central Kaş, this 4th-century BC sarcophagus has weathered Lycian inscriptions on its faces and a Gothic-arched lid topped with reliefs of lions. Locals walked past it for centuries before it was identified.

Phellos on the ridge

On the high ground above Çukurbağ, the ruins of Phellos are wilder and less visited: a small theatre, a Hellenistic temple, cisterns, fortification walls, and — most striking — a "house tomb" carved out of free-standing rock with a bull-relief on one side. The walk up is the best way to understand the original geography: a defensible high city that needed a sea outlet, and grew one.

Function in the Lycian League

Under the federation Antiphellos held one vote in the Lycian League's assembly — the lowest tier, alongside other small members like Aperlai and Simena. Patara and Xanthos held three each. Despite the low political weight, the city was clearly wealthy by the Roman period, when most of the visible monumental tombs were carved.

Nature and setting

Kaş sits at the foot of the Akdağlar (the "White Mountains"), which rise sharply behind the town to over 3,000 metres. The slopes are classic Mediterranean maquis — kermes oak, lentisk, myrtle, wild olive, oregano — with patches of Calabrian pine where the ground is deeper.

Offshore, the Kaş–Kekova Special Environmental Protection Area protects a long stretch of coast that includes posidonia meadows, several known Mediterranean monk seal caves, and one of the highest underwater biodiversity counts in Turkey. The harbour itself is one of the most consistent diving sites in the country.

Cool small things

  • The Greek island of Meis, visible from anywhere in Kaş, is closer to Antiphellos (2 km) than it is to any other point on Greek territory (160 km). Day-boats still run across.
  • The Lion Sarcophagus you walk past on Uzun Çarşı has lost the lid of its hyposorion (lower chamber) — a thief in antiquity opened it from below rather than trying to lift the heavy upper lid.
  • The unusual word antí in Antiphellos doesn't mean "against" — it means "facing." There are several other "Anti-" cities in antiquity (Antipatra, Antiochia ad Cragum) named the same way.
  • Some of the cut stones in the older parts of Kaş — including parts of the seafront wall — are clearly reused Lycian blocks. If you look at the corner of an alley wall in the Old Town, you may be looking at a 2,300-year-old quarry mark.
  • The annual Likya (Lycian) cultural festival in Kaş takes place each summer and includes performances inside the ancient theatre.

Modern Kaş

A small town of about 8,000 year-round residents, ringed by guesthouses, sea-kayaking outfits, dive shops, and a handful of restaurants that close nothing earlier than midnight in July. Kaş is one of the few places on the coast where the Old Town survived the 1970s development boom largely intact, and the streets are narrow, white-washed, and bougainvillea-heavy. The Saturday open market is one of the better on the coast.

Practical

Getting there. 3–4 hours by bus from Antalya, 2 hours from Fethiye. The Lycian Way passes directly through. Kaş itself is walkable.
Best season. May, June, September and October. July and August are hot and crowded. Winters are mild but rainy.
Don't miss. A late-afternoon swim from the small beach below the theatre, with the ruins lit gold and Meis turning lavender.
