Ecology of the Lycian coast

Maquis, posidonia, monk seal, loggerhead

Lycia is a Mediterranean biodiversity hotspot in miniature. Within a single 50-kilometre stretch you can move from posidonia meadows on the seabed, through fire-adapted pine, into kermes-oak maquis, up to subalpine meadows on the limestone peaks. This page collects the most distinctive — and most threatened — ecosystems and species of the coast.

Mediterranean maquis

Maquis is the dense, evergreen, fragrant shrubland that covers most of the Lycian hillsides up to about 800 m. Its key species are kermes oak, lentisk, myrtle, strawberry tree, wild olive, carob, and the climber smilax. Lower-growing matorral takes over on the most exposed and stony slopes — rosemary, oregano, sage, thyme, and cistus. The smell of a maquis hillside at noon in May is one of the defining sensations of Lycia.

Calabrian pine and fire

The dominant tree of the coastal forests is Pinus brutia, the Calabrian or Turkish red pine, a fire-adapted species whose cones often require heat to open. The catastrophic wildfires of 2021 along this coast burned through Manavgat, Marmaris, and parts of the eastern Lycian foothills; recovery is ongoing and studied closely. Where regeneration is allowed, pine seedlings re-establish quickly — where it is replaced by goats or development, the ecosystem can be lost.

Sea: posidonia, monk seal, loggerhead

Posidonia meadows

The Mediterranean's most important seagrass, Posidonia oceanica, stabilises the seabed, oxygenates the coastal water, sequesters carbon, and provides nursery habitat for most of the fish you'd eat for dinner. The meadows around Kekova and Kaş are extensive but declining due to anchor damage. Anchor only on sand.

Mediterranean monk seal

Fewer than 800 individuals of Monachus monachus remain worldwide. A small population uses sea caves along the central Lycian coast, particularly around Kekova and the cliffs between Kaş and Demre. Sightings are rare and should not be sought — flushing a pup from a cave can be fatal to it.

Loggerhead turtles

Caretta caretta nest on several Lycian beaches, with the most important sites at Patara, Çıralı, and Dalyan/İztuzu (just west of Lycia proper). Patara's 18-kilometre beach is closed to humans between sunset and sunrise from May to October. Lights of any kind disorient hatchlings; flash photography is illegal.

Highland endemics

The Akdağlar above Kaş and the Bey Dağları behind Antalya reach over 3,000 m and hold a number of plants endemic to southwestern Anatolia, including several Crocus, Iris, and Verbascum species. The Lycian fir (Abies cilicica ssp. isaurica) holds the highest forests.

Ongoing initiatives

  • Kaş–Kekova SEPA — Special Environmental Protection Area covering 260 km² of coast and sea.
  • SAD-AFAG — Mediterranean Monk Seal Research Group, active on the central coast.
  • DEKAFOK — Sea turtle monitoring on Lycian nesting beaches.
  • Posidonia mooring projects — replacing anchor damage with fixed eco-moorings in busy bays.
  • 2021 wildfire restoration — long-term reforestation and natural regeneration monitoring.
